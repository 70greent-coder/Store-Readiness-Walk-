Microgreenz · cut fresh from the tray

Tiny greens. Serious nutrition.

Microgreens are vegetables and herbs harvested at their first leaves, about 1–3 weeks after planting. Government and university researchers have found that many of them carry several times the vitamins of the full-grown plant.

What you're getting

Harvested at the moment a plant is most concentrated.

A seed packs everything a plant needs to get started. At the first-leaf stage, that store of nutrients sits in a few tender bites, before it spreads out into a full-sized vegetable.

    Seed

    Seeds are sown densely on trays of growing medium.

  2. Days 2–4

    Germination

    Roots anchor and stems push up toward the light.

  3. Days 5–9

    Greening

    Under light, stems color up and the first leaves open.

  4. Days 7–21

    Harvest

    Cut just above the soil when the first leaves are fully open.

The research

What scientists have found so far

Each card links to the original study. We say clearly which results come from lab or animal work, since human trials on microgreens are still early.

Lab analysis · 25 varieties
4–40×

Microgreens out-measured mature leaves for vitamins C, E, K and carotenoids

USDA and University of Maryland scientists tested 25 commercial microgreens. Red cabbage led for vitamin C, cilantro for carotenoids, garnet amaranth for vitamin K and green daikon radish for vitamin E. Cilantro microgreens had about three times the beta-carotene of mature cilantro.

Xiao Z, Lester GE, Luo Y, Wang Q. Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 60(31):7644–7651, 2012. doi:10.1021/jf300459b

Animal study · 8 weeks
↓ LDL

Red cabbage microgreens lowered cholesterol and liver inflammation in mice

Mice on a high-fat diet that also ate red cabbage microgreens had lower LDL, less cholesterol stored in the liver and fewer inflammation markers. The microgreens held more polyphenols and glucosinolates than mature red cabbage.

Huang H, Jiang X, Xiao Z, et al. Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 64(48):9161–9171, 2016. PubMed 27933986

Lab analysis · broccoli
20–50×

Young broccoli carries far more glucoraphanin than the grown-up head

Johns Hopkins researchers found three-day-old broccoli sprouts held 20 to 50 times the protective compounds of mature broccoli. The main one, glucoraphanin, turns into sulforaphane when you chew. Microgreens are a little older than sprouts, and they're part of the same young-plant story.

Fahey JW, Zhang Y, Talalay P. PNAS 94(19):10367–10372, 1997. doi:10.1073/pnas.94.19.10367

Review · 2023
Rich in polyphenols

Reviews point to antioxidants that may support long-term health

Recent scientific reviews describe microgreens as concentrated sources of polyphenols, carotenoids, vitamins and minerals. They also note that most of the health evidence so far comes from lab and animal studies, so more human research is needed.

Bhaswant M, et al. Molecules 28(2):867, 2023. PMC9864543

Field guide to the tray

In the kitchen

A handful on everything

Microgreens are best raw, so they keep their crunch and their vitamins. Add them at the end.

Breakfast

Pile onto scrambled eggs, avocado toast or a breakfast burrito.

Smoothies

Blend a handful of broccoli or pea shoots in with fruit. You won't taste them.

Sandwiches & wraps

Swap lettuce for radish microgreens for a peppery crunch.

Tacos & bowls

Cilantro and red cabbage brighten tacos, rice bowls and pho.

Salads

Toss with greens or serve on their own with lemon and olive oil.

Kids' plates

Sweet pea shoots and sunflower are an easy first green for picky eaters.
